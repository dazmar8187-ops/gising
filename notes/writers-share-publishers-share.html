Writer's share and publisher's share, in plain English

Songwriting money is confusing on purpose. It doesn't have to be. Here's the simple version.

Darryl Saunders · 10 October 2026

When a song you wrote is played on the radio, streamed, performed live or used in a video, it earns money for the song itself, separately from whoever owns the recording. That money is usually thought of in two halves.

The two halves

HalfWho it's forWho usually collects it
Writer's shareThe people who wrote the songYour collecting society (FILSCAP in the Philippines), paid straight to you
Publisher's shareWhoever publishes the song: a publisher, or you if you don't have oneA publisher, or your society if you're registered as your own publisher

The details vary by country, society and type of income, but the shape is the same almost everywhere: one half belongs to the writers, and the other half belongs to the publisher.

The writer's share is yours

The writer's share is the part that should never be sold or signed away. It's paid to the writers by their society, split between them the way the song was split. If three of you wrote a song equally, each of you gets a third of the writer's share.

What a publisher does for its share

A good publisher earns the publisher's share by working for the song: registering it everywhere it needs to be registered, collecting money from countries and services you'd never chase yourself, and pitching it to artists, films, adverts and games. A publisher that only collects, and never pitches, is charging for paperwork.

If you don't have a publisher

Then you are your own publisher, and the publisher's share belongs to you too. Whether you can collect it easily depends on your society and how you've registered. Ask your society how to register as both writer and publisher, or how they handle writers without one. Some money goes uncollected every year simply because nobody registered the song properly.

Where Gising Sining House fits

For songs written together in the Room or at a camp, the house holds 10% of the publishing share. The writers keep all of the writer's share and the other 90% of the publishing. In return, the house registers the songs and pitches the best ones. You can read exactly how that works in How to split a song fairly.

One more thing: the recording is separate. Everything above is about the song (the composition). The recording of the song (the master) earns its own money, which goes to whoever owns the recording, often the artist or a label. A writer who also releases their own recordings earns from both.

This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice. Rules differ by country and society; check with yours.
