How to split a song fairly (and why we sign before we write)

Most fights between cowriters aren't about money. They're about assumptions nobody said out loud. Here's how to get the split right before the first line.

Darryl Saunders · 10 October 2026

Two people write a song in an afternoon. A year later it's on a playlist, money starts arriving, and one of them says, "I thought it was 70/30. The hook was mine." The other thought it was even. Neither is lying. They just never said it.

That's why in this house we agree the split before we write, not after. It feels awkward the first time. By the third cowrite it takes two minutes and nobody notices.

The default: split it equally

Most professional writing rooms split equally between everyone in the room. Three writers, a third each. Two writers, half each. It doesn't matter who wrote more lines.

That sounds unfair until you've done it a few times. The line that made the song often only came because of the bad idea someone else threw out ten minutes earlier. Counting words or syllables rewards the wrong things and makes people guard their ideas instead of sharing them. Equal splits keep the room generous.

When a different split makes sense

Equal is a default, not a rule. Writers sometimes agree something else when:

  • someone brings a nearly finished song and the others help with one section;
  • someone joins for only part of the session;
  • a producer builds the track but doesn't write melody or lyric, and everyone agrees how that counts.

Any split is fine if everyone agrees it out loud, before or right at the start, and writes it down. The only bad split is the one people find out about later.

What goes on a split sheet

A split sheet is a one-page record of who wrote the song and how it's shared. Keep it simple:

  • the song title (a working title is fine) and the date;
  • every writer's full legal name, and what they did (lyric, melody, music);
  • each writer's collecting society (FILSCAP or their home society) and IPI number, if they have one;
  • each writer's publisher, if they have one;
  • the percentages, which must add up to 100%;
  • everyone's signature.

Writer's share and publishing are different things. A song earns money in two halves: the writer's share and the publisher's share. Your split sheet should say how each is divided. If those words are new, read Writer's share and publisher's share, in plain English.

How Gising Sining House does it

Every cowrite in the Room or at a camp starts with an ownership form in the writing room, signed before anyone writes. For a song written by three people:

ShareWriter 1Writer 2Writer 3The house
Writer's sharea thirda thirda thirdnothing
Publishing30%30%30%10%

The writer's share stays entirely with the writers. The house holds 10% of the publishing, which pays for registering the song and pitching the best ones to artists, brands and sync. If the song never earns, the house earns nothing from it. Songs you write on your own stay 100% yours.

If you've already written songs with nothing on paper

Do it this week, while everyone is still friends. Message your cowriters, suggest an even split unless you all remember it differently, and sign a sheet. It's much easier now than after a song earns something.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. For anything with real money at stake, talk to a music lawyer.
